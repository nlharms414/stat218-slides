Section 5.3

Comparing Two Groups: Theory-Based Approach

Section 5.3 Learning Objectives

  • Identify when a theory-based approach can be used to find a p-value or confidence interval when comparing two categorical variables.

  • Understand how confidence level and sample size impact the confidence interval for the difference between two proportions.

Recap

Comparing Two Groups

What are we interested in?

  • Parameter: The difference in long run proportions between two groups

    • \pi_{Group\:1} - \pi_{Group\:2}

We estimate the parameter using:

  • Observed Statistic: The difference in conditional proportions

    • \hat{p}_{Group\:1} - \hat{p}_{Group\:2}

Hypotheses for Two Groups

  • Null Hypothesis: There is no difference between the long run proportions of two groups

  • Alt. Hypothesis: There is a difference between the long run proportions of two groups

    • Difference can be greater than, less than, or not equal to

Hypotheses for Two Groups

In symbols:

  • H_0: \pi_{Group\:1} - \pi_{Group\:2} = 0

  • H_A: \pi_{Group\:1} - \pi_{Group\:2} >,<,\neq 0

Equivalently, we could also write:

  • H_0: \pi_{Group\:1}=\pi_{Group\:2}

  • H_A: \pi_{Group\:1}>,<,\neq\pi_{Group\:2}

Strength of Evidence

Determining Strength of Evidence

Two broad ways to get strength of evidence:

  • Simulation

  • Theory-based approach

Through either of the above two can find:

  • P-value

  • Standardized statistic

  • Confidence interval

Strength of Evidence Using Simulation

For two groups, the Applet does the following to simulate:

  • Study data is input

  • All responses (success/failure) from the study are put on “cards”

  • For one study simulation, cards are randomly split into the two explanatory groups

  • Conditional proportions are calculated for each group

Strength of Evidence Using Simulation

Consider the data from the Murderous Nurse example.

  • 1,641 total shifts were analyzed

  • 74 of the total shifts at least one person died

  • Gilbert worked 257 of the total shifts, and did not work 1384 of them

Strength of Evidence Using Simulation

To simulate this, we would:

  1. Get 1641 cards

  2. 74 of the cards would have the response that at least one person died

    • The remaining 1567 would have the response that no one died

  3. To simulate one “trial” the cards would be shuffled and randomly split into two groups of 257 and 1384

    • These groups represent Gilbert/Non-Gilbert shifts!

Strength of Evidence Using Simulation

  1. Calculate conditional proportions for each group

    • Proportion of times at least one person died

  2. Take the difference between the conditional proportions and plot on the null distribution

  3. Repeat lots of times!

Then, we compare our actual observed statistic to the null distribution.

Strength of Evidence Using Theory-Based Approach

  • If we meet validity conditions, we can use theory

  • Using theory we can obtain:

    • P-value (still use the Applet)

    • Standardized statistic

    • Confidence interval

Validity Conditions for Theory-Based Approach

To use the theory-based approach for two groups:

  • Each box in the two-way table needs at least 10 observations

  • This means each explanatory group needs at least 10 successes and 10 failures

    • Similar condition to one proportion

Standardized Statistic for Two Groups

General standardized statistic (SS) formula:

SS=\frac{Observed\: Statistic - Hypothesized\: Value}{Standard\: Error\: under\: the\: null}

When comparing two groups:

z=\frac{(\hat{p}_{1} - \hat{p}_{2})-0}{\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})(\frac{1}{n_1}+\frac{1}{n_2})}}

Standardized Statistic for Two Groups

Note that the standard error for the previous statistic uses the combined proportion of success (\hat{p})

\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})(\frac{1}{n_1}+\frac{1}{n_2})}

Under the null, we assume \hat{p}_1=\hat{p}_2, so we estimate with \hat{p}, the pooled proportion of success

  • This is also referred to as pooled standard error

Standardized Statistic Interpretation

  • Interpretation remains essentially the same

  • “\hat{p}_1 is ___ standard deviations away from \hat{p}_2”

Confidence Interval for Two Groups

General confidence interval formula:

CI=Observed\: Statistic\: ± Multiplier*SE

When comparing two groups:

CI=(\hat{p}_1-\hat{p}_2)±2*\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}_1(1-\hat{p}_1)}{n_1}+\frac{\hat{p}_2(1-\hat{p}_2)}{n_2}}

Confidence Interval for Two Groups

Notice that our SE formula differs slightly

\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}_1(1-\hat{p}_1)}{n_1}+\frac{\hat{p}_2(1-\hat{p}_2)}{n_2}}

Now, we are assuming that \hat{p}_1≠\hat{p}_2, so we no longer use the pooled standard error.

Confidence Interval Interpretation

  • “We are 95% confident that the long run difference in proportion of context between group 1 and group 2 falls between lower bound and upper bound.”

Alternatively, we can say:

  • “We’re 95% confident that the true proportion of context in group 1 is lower bound to upper bound more/less than the true proportion of context in group 2.”

Near-Sightedness Example

  • Thought to be an association between myopia (near-sightedness) and use of nightlights as a child

  • 479 children were observed

  • For each child, two questions were recorded:

    • Did the child sleep with light in the room or total darkness before the age of 2?

    • At the child’s most recent eye exam, were they near-sighted or not?

Near-Sightedness Example

  • Explanatory variable: Sleeping with light or not as a child

  • Response variable: Near-sighted or not

  • Parameter of interest: Difference in long run proportions of near-sightedness between kids who slept in total darkness and those who slept with a light before the age of 2. (\pi_D-\pi_L)

Setting up the hypotheses

  • H_0: \pi_D-\pi_L=0

  • H_A: \pi_D-\pi_L < 0

Here, we use “less than” since we hypothesize that kids who slept in darkness have a lower rate of myopia.

  • Equivalent to: H_A: \pi_D<\pi_L

Near-Sightedness Two-Way Table


Dark Light Total
Near-sighted 18 119 137
Not near-sighted 154 188 342
Total 172 307 479

Do we meet the criteria for using the theory-based approach?

Calculating the Standardized Statistic


Dark Light Total
Near-sighted 18 119 137
Not near-sighted 154 188 342
Total 172 307 479
  • n_D=172
  • n_L=307
  • \hat{p}=\frac{137}{479}=0.29
  • \hat{p}_D=\frac{18}{172}=0.10
  • \hat{p}_L=\frac{119}{307}=0.39

Calculating the Standardized Statistic

z=\frac{\hat{p}_{D} - \hat{p}_{L}}{\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})(\frac{1}{n_D}+\frac{1}{n_L})}}

=\frac{0.10 - 0.39}{\sqrt{0.29(1-0.29)(\frac{1}{172}+\frac{1}{307})}}

=-6.71

Calculating the Confidence Interval

CI=(\hat{p}_D-\hat{p}_L)±2*\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}_D(1-\hat{p}_D)}{n_D}+\frac{\hat{p}_L(1-\hat{p}_L)}{n_L}}

=(0.10-0.39)±2*\sqrt{\frac{0.10*0.90}{172}+\frac{0.39*0.61}{307}}

=(-0.362,-0.218)

Interpreting the Confidence Interval

“We are 95% confident that the long run difference in proportions of near-sightedness between children who slept in darkness and children who slept with light is between -0.362 and -0.218.”

Interpreting the Confidence Interval

Alternatively, we could also say:


“We are 95% confident that the true proportion of near-sightedness in children who slept in darkness is 0.218 to 0.362 lower than the true proportion of near-sightedness in children who slept with a light.”

Drawing Conclusions

Recall our hypotheses:

  • H_0: \pi_D-\pi_L=0

  • H_A: \pi_D-\pi_L<0

Standardized statistic and confidence interval:

  • z=-6.71

  • CI=(-0.362,-0.218)

  • Note that both point to the same conclusion: reject H_0

Drawing Conclusions

“We have strong evidence to conclude that the long run difference in proportions of near-sightedness between kids who slept in a dark room is lower than that for kids who slept with a light in their room.”
